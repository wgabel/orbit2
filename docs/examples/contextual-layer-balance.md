# Contextual layer balance — melody, accents and accompaniment

[Architecture](../../ORBIT2-ARCHITECTURE.md) · [Production workflow](mixing-mastering-and-analysis.md) · [Psychoacoustics](music-theory-and-sound-design.md#6-perceived-loudness-gain-staging-and-dynamic-range) · [Composition](generative-ambient-composition.md)

**Purpose:** help an agent mix individual musical parts so they remain audible and expressive without overpowering the intended scene. This is a focused extension of the production guide, not a new DSP feature, automatic mastering algorithm, required graph template or universal set of volume targets. Read it when relative layer levels or musical clarity matter; use the appropriate depth for the task.

## 1. Balance is a relationship, not a fader setting

A foreground melody can be too loud in a breakdown and too quiet in a climax with exactly the same gain. The appropriate relationship depends on the role, harmonic register, waveform, envelope, competing parts, effects, section density and listening conditions. A level that worked for one patch is only a starting hypothesis for another.

| Musical role | What to preserve | Common failure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Principal melody | Readable motif, note attacks, phrase dynamics and emotional focus | Every note jumps out of an otherwise spacious mix; or disappears in dense sections. |
| Secondary melody / answer | Recognizable detail, contrast and moments of dialogue | Competes equally with the lead throughout, or becomes inaudible decoration. |
| Harmony / atmosphere | Emotional color, space, coherent background and evolving movement | Sustained midrange or wet tails obscure the notes that carry the phrase. |
| Bass / rhythm | Foundation, groove, accents and intended impact | Loud sub consumes headroom; mid-bass harmonics or transients drown out the rest. |

Roles may change during a piece: an industrial drone can be the foreground in the intro, a bass call can take over during a breakdown, and a melody can intentionally disappear. Some styles want an unusually dominant lead, massive bass or near-inaudible background. Respect the brief, reference character, and listener feedback; do not force all roles or genres into equal loudness.

## 2. Research and production evidence

These sources offer complementary evidence, not Orbit settings or universal prescriptions. Technical standards define measurement; listening research explores perception and preferences; professional engineers describe practice in particular contexts.

| Source | Finding and practical implication |
| --- | --- |
| [ISO 226:2023 — normal equal-loudness contours](https://www.iso.org/standard/83117.html) | Equal perceived loudness for pure tones varies with frequency and reproduction SPL. A sub, reed and metallic accent with equal RMS need not sound equally loud. Check at consistent comfortable monitoring levels, then at a second level/playback system when possible. The pure-tone curves are not a mastering EQ target. |
| [ITU-R BS.1770-5, Annex 1](https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-BS.1770-5-202311-I) and [EBU Tech 3341](https://tech.ebu.ch/publications/tech3341/) | K-weighted mean-square energy, channel weights and integrated gating underpin programme loudness; EBU specifies 400 ms momentary and 3 s short-term windows. Use consistent windows to locate problems, but a stem/rest ratio is a custom diagnostic, not a standardized loudness-balance or masking meter. |
| [De Man, Leonard, King & Reiss, *An Analysis and Evaluation of Audio Features for Multitrack Music Mixtures*, ISMIR 2014, §4.2](https://qmro.qmul.ac.uk/xmlui/bitstream/handle/123456789/12588/De%20Man%20AN%20ANALYSIS%20AND%20EVALUATION%202014%20Published.pdf) | In an experiment on eight songs and multiple mixes per song, relative loudness depended on material and some instrument levels differed by engineer. Vocals were typically more prominent than the other studied sources. The reported vocal mean (−2.7 ± 1.6 LU relative to the **whole mix**, with the study's modified loudness feature) is descriptive of that dataset, not a synth-lead target or a ratio against the *rest* of a mix. |
| [Mansbridge, Finn & Reiss, *Implementation and Evaluation of Autonomous Multi-track Fader Control*, AES 2012, §6](https://www.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/~josh/documents/2012/MansbridgeFinnReiss-AES1322012-AutoMultitrackFaders.pdf) | Their loudness-driven fader method uses activity gating and smoothing to avoid some unintended gain changes. They report a tambourine-dominated failure when matching individual source loudness. Group related percussion/voices and make deliberate role choices; do not equalize every track or boost rests. |
| [Ward, Reiss & Athwal, *Multi-track mixing using a model of loudness and partial loudness*, AES 2012](https://www.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/~josh/documents/2012/WardReissAthwal-AES133-Multitrackmixingusingamodelofloudnessandpartialloudness.pdf) | Listening in isolation and within a mixture are different: their multiband auditory model estimates a source's loudness with and without concurrent maskers and motivates time-varying gain. They call for further musical validation. A K-weighted energy ratio alone is much simpler and cannot estimate partial loudness, detect all masking or guarantee intelligibility. |
| [Ronan et al., *Automatic Minimisation of Masking in Multitrack Audio using Subgroups*, 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09960) | Their masking-reduction metric, spectral/dynamic processing and subgrouping were evaluated with listening tests; results suggest improved clarity/quality in the studied material. This motivates role grouping and local spectral diagnostics. The goal is not to eliminate all overlap: deliberately blended harmonics, texture and wetness can be musical. |
| [Chris Lord-Alge interview, Sound On Sound, *Welcome To The Black Parade* mix](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/secrets-mix-engineers-chris-lord-alge) and [*Mixing Essentials*, Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/mixing-essentials) | Establish a rough balance using important anchor tracks, work with all the instruments audible, and use fader rides to help changing sections develop. Mixing in context and automation protect a musical arc better than only fixing static solo levels. |
| [Nick Messitte, iZotope — reference tracks when mixing](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/13-tips-for-using-references-while-mixing) | Compare references after compensating playback level differences. A louder mastered reference can otherwise bias judgements about tone or compression. Choose references for separate relevant qualities, not a single mandatory spectral or loudness curve. |

## 3. Practical mix decisions in Orbit

Start from the musical intention and an anchor (e.g. drone, kick/sub or main motif). Adjust other layers around that relationship while listening to the full mix. A quick solo can diagnose source timbre, but an isolated sound being attractive does not mean its level, resonance or wetness works with other parts.

| Diagnostic observation | Possible response to test |
| --- | --- |
| Lead is intrusive only when drums or bass leave | Smoothly attenuate the exposed phrases; leave denser sections alone. Retain note-to-note accents and resolution. |
| Lead is broadly too quiet or lacks body | Check its register, harmonics, articulation and routing first; then increase local trim where needed. A hollow timbre is not fixed just by gain. |
| Lead is audible in solo but unclear in one busy section | Inspect simultaneous spectra and note onsets. Try a small targeted bell cut in the competing voice, different register/voicing, lighter wet return, shorter tail or staggered events. Recheck context before turning the lead up. |
| A few notes or accents are piercing | Inspect brightness/resonance, short-window peak and envelope; reduce only the offending attack, velocity, overtone or phrase. Avoid dulling the entire piece. |
| Tiny background accents disappear | Check intended role and active windows. Lift selectively in dense sections, or make room by lowering a masker. Keep them subordinate when that is intended. |
| Echoes accumulate or compete with the next motif | Control feedback, damping, decay, wet level or send/excitation; measure transitions including the surviving tail. |

Use separate voice/composite groups and meaningful aliases, independent local trim and section-level controls. A suggested order is source/envelope → voice shaping → **pre-effect arrangement gain** (controls excitation, leaves existing tails) → delay/reverb → **post-effect trim** or weighted root Mixer connection (controls the entire voice including tails) → root master Gain. Do not change the input of a deliberately nonlinear harmonic experiment merely to change its output level; adjust downstream when preserving the interaction. A small local EQ adjustment may be more useful than a gain increase. [Effects](../audio/effects.md) and [musical techniques](genres/orbit-techniques.md) describe currently supported options. Current Gain is linear and bounded to 0–1; automation is not a detector-driven compressor. Use supported modulation and transport controls, e.g. a stepped Note Sequencer level score with positive glide into a Gain; separate it from pitch, note rest/velocity and articulation lanes. Verify timing, control units, bounds and available sequencer capacity in the current app. Use explicit zero level for actual rests (pitch Hold does not mute).

For authored automation, prefer small, gradual changes on phrases/transitions; listen for pumping, flattening of expressive accents and sudden tail cuts. If a ratio is 3 dB above a chosen *section-specific* diagnostic objective, a first approximation for a post-effect linear attenuation is `10^(-3/20) ≈ 0.708` of the old amplitude. If gain is upstream of nonlinear DSP or before an effect with memory, this arithmetic is not a prediction of the resulting ratio. Re-render. A gain increase above an existing unity stage may require room in earlier trims or an appropriate supported routing design, not inventing a Gain > 1.

## 4. Validation — simultaneous windowed role ratios

Use the method below when feasible, especially on full compositions or after feedback about competing melody levels. It is descriptive and can be adapted by genre, tempo, instrumentation and available tools; it does not require creating a new app meter or an external renderer for every edit.

1. **Define roles and intended activity.** Identify the lead, secondary motif/accents, accompaniment and sectional focus (who should lead when?). Mark intended pauses and solo features. Identify comparable sparse/dense passages including the transitions. Set diagnostic bounds only if they make sense for this brief, or compare to the initial balance without a preset target.
2. **Capture aligned audio.** Prefer native execution when available. Preserve original score, phase/time, gains, random seeds if controllable and effect history. Capture the actual signal of each role after relevant effects, automation, connection weights and final linear gain from the same running score. Sum grouped roles as sample arrays with correct alignment and channel conventions. If nonlinear shared bus effects are present, contributions may not add independently; label a pre-bus analysis and measure the complete post-bus master separately. A counterfactual mute test can be informative but is not necessarily an additive stem. Verify summed stems reconstruct the intended linear bus/master within numerical tolerance; check the capture point. Independent, approximate renders and solo captures are not proof of native output.
3. **Compare time windows.** Use 400 ms (events/accents; e.g. hop 200 ms) and 3 s (phrase/context; e.g. hop 1 s), plus musically meaningful bar/section windows or shorter transient tests. K-weight signals continuously from the same start (no per-window filter resets); use correct sampling-rate coefficients. For mono, define `E(x,w) = mean(K(x)[w]^2)`; define the role/backing difference `D(w) = 10 log10(E(role,w) / E(rest,w))`. This is a K-weighted energy ratio in dB, not dBFS, gated LUFS, partial loudness or a measure of subjective audibility. For lead use `rest = accent + accompaniment`; for accent use `rest = lead + accompaniment`; also assess `(lead + accent)` against accompaniment and optionally nearby spectral competitors. Sum **samples before squaring**; correlations/cancellation matter. Never sum stem dB values or use the full mix as the denominator without stating it contains the target. For multichannel measurements, use consistent channel weighting and arrangement, not arbitrary mono downmixing.
4. **Keep silence meaningful.** Use the score/activity masks where possible, with a floor to exclude numerical silence. When using an energy gate, choose and disclose it; e.g. role weighted level above max(−58 dB re unit mean-square energy, role's strongest same-size window minus 24 dB), backing above −58 dB. These are experiment settings, not BS.1770 gates. Log intentionally exposed solo/rest/tail windows separately; if backing is silent, the ratio is undefined rather than a reason to turn the solo down. For A/B, freeze the same score-defined or baseline-selected window indices and thresholds; additionally inspect newly active/faded windows in either version so a new problem is not masked by selection. Report window counts and gate values, and label any per-render-relative selection.
5. **Summarize and diagnose.** Report median, P10, P90, optionally max and timestamps, counts/fractions above a brief-specific flag (e.g. 0 dB if accompaniment should remain a substantial part of the sound). P90 is the 90th percentile threshold, not the mean of the loudest 10%. Compare sections by window center (or clearly specified placement); locate individual offending windows, note whether an intended lead entrance is responsible. A lower median with a better P90 can be good or bad depending on intelligibility. Inspect raw peak, unweighted RMS, short-time spectrum and competing frequency bands where relevant; the single weighted ratio cannot see frequency/time masking, note identity, tail interference or listener differences.
6. **Refine and verify.** Do not blindly normalize all tracks to the same loudness, maximize the ratio, suppress every positive ratio, or boost intentional silence. Preserve successful sections. Make targeted level, tone, rhythm or wet/dry changes; compare the same windows and actual meter peaks/true peak/LUFS/LRA on the master, plus end/loop. Audition critical excerpts in the whole mix and A/B at matched presentation level without changing intra-mix ratios. When actual listening is unavailable, provide a preview for human review and label measured improvements as hypotheses about sound quality. A short transient intentionally ahead of the backing can be musically essential even if the 3 s ratio is negative.

Lightweight calculation outline (mono, aligned linear stems, illustrative):

```text
lead_k = K_weight(lead_samples, sample_rate)
rest_k = K_weight(sum(aligned_other_stem_samples), sample_rate)
for each complete window w:
    A = mean(lead_k[w]^2)
    B = mean(rest_k[w]^2)
    if intended_active(w) and A, B above diagnostic floor:
        ratio_db[w] = 10 * log10(A / B)
compare same window indices and percentile statistics between renders
```

For reproduction at 48 kHz, the two serial biquads in BS.1770-5 Annex 1 Tables 1–2 specify the coefficients used here. Preserve filter state across blocks. For other sampling rates obtain coefficients with the appropriate frequency response or use a verified library. Validate the analysis against known cases (equal nonzero signals → 0 dB; scaling one by 0.5 → −6.02 dB; silent backing → undefined; identical aligned stems doubled → +6.02 dB, opposite-polarity stems cancel). Standard programme measurements should still use a verified loudness/true-peak meter; [production guide §2](mixing-mastering-and-analysis.md#2-what-to-measure-and-what-each-measurement-cannot-tell-you) gives FFmpeg examples. In-app [signal observation](../audio/signal-observation.md) offers short peak/RMS and spectrum observations, not this exact synchronized offline analysis.

## 5. Measured case — Submerged Relay (three minutes)

Original 100 BPM D-minor industrial ambient / psybient / IDM / dub techno / neurobass composition: 25 scenes of 7.2 s, three-bar phrases, eight musical voice groups. A 36 s lead motif, independent 15-eighth-note accent cycle, wet chord/atmospheric parts and changing bass/percussion density create deliberately unequal melodic roles. The listener's preceding feedback was that some melodies were too loud, but should not become too quiet. This case is an **independent 48 kHz mono render**, not a native engine recording or listening-validated success.

We captured the lead, accent and summed six other voices in the same running renderer, with actual root mixer weights and master fade, checking that the three stems summed to the master to within 1e−6 sample error. Windows: 400 ms/200 ms hop and 3 s/1 s hop. Applied continuous BS.1770 K-weighting and the energy gate described in §4. Baseline exposed melodies in the breakdown and outro; accents were comparatively weak in dense passages. Changes were limited to musical gain controls: lead root weight 1.00 → 0.94 plus a more restrained scene envelope in exposed passages and a lift on the bass return; accent root weight 0.90 → 1.00, body/flash envelope amounts 0.19 → 0.38 and 0.047 → 0.080, and stronger dense-section scene levels. The arrangement, harmony and rhythm were retained. Scene gain is before the local delay/reverb, so effect-tail behavior is included in the resulting measurements.

The following is a **paired** reanalysis using the same baseline-active window indices in both renders (3 s; 1 s hop). The original delivery used separately selected active windows; its main lead median was −8.34 → −8.81 dB and combined melody P90 −2.54 → −3.64 dB. The paired analysis controls for changes in selection caused by editing the levels:

| Role vs simultaneous rest | Active windows | Median initial → revised (dB) | P90 initial → revised (dB) | Windows > 0 dB initial → revised |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Main melody | 148 | −8.34 → −8.87 | −2.70 → −4.77 | 5 → 0 |
| Secondary accents | 174 | −25.07 → −15.41 | −17.46 → −11.01 | 0 → 0 |
| Both melodies | 155 | −8.37 → −8.07 | −2.54 → −3.54 | 5 → 0 |

With identical 400 ms baseline-selected windows, main melody P90 changed −0.87 → −2.37 dB (45 → 23 of 594 windows above 0 dB); combined melodies P90 −0.88 → −1.79 dB (46 → 35 of 633 above 0 dB). Brief foreground emphasis survived. Per-render selection in the final render included 174 active 3 s combined-melody windows, of which none exceeded 0 dB. In the original per-render analysis, combined melody section medians improved from −22.08 to −15.41 dB in the pressure groove (57.6–86.4 s), with the lead largely resting; the flooded void/breakdown (86.4–108 s) fell from −4.72 to −6.19 dB, while the return (108–144 s) rose from −10.52 to −8.45 dB. The intent was to even out *unwanted* prominence without flattening form.

Final independent PCM: 180 s, sample peak −3.56 dBFS, RMS −21.62 dBFS. FFmpeg measured the decoded MP3 at −23.8 LUFS-I (single mono), 11.8 LU LRA and estimated −3.8 dBTP. The 141-node graph passed 12,696 scheduler boundary/hold checks; these are timing/structure checks, not native waveform or perceptual validation. No limiter or external dynamics treatment was applied. Neither the negative ratios nor zero 3 s flags define a generally good mix; brief exposed melodies may be intentional and different styles may call for entirely different relationships. The case supports a reusable process: **measure both ends of the problem, preserve intentional dynamics, and validate the same musical moment before and after a targeted change.** Human audition remains the final check.
